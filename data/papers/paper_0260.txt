Title: Recent Advances towards Safe, Responsible, and Moral Dialogue Systems: A Survey

Recent Advances towards Safe, Responsible, and Moral
Dialogue Systems: A Survey
JIAWEN DENG∗, HAO SUN∗, ZHEXIN ZHANG, JIALE CHENG, and MINLIE HUANG, De-
partment of Computer Science and Technology, Institute for Artificial Intelligence, Beijing National Research
Center for Information Science and Technology, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China
With the development of artificial intelligence, dialogue systems have been endowed with amazing chit-
chat capabilities, and there is widespread interest and discussion about whether the generated contents
are socially beneficial. In this paper, we present a new perspective of research scope towards building a
safe, responsible, and modal dialogue system, including 1) abusive and toxic contents, 2) unfairness and
discrimination, 3) ethics and morality issues, and 4) risk of misleading and privacy information. Besides, we
review the mainstream methods for evaluating the safety of large models from the perspectives of exposure
and detection of safety issues. The recent advances in methodologies for the safety improvement of both
end-to-end dialogue systems and pipeline-based models are further introduced. Finally, we discussed six
existing challenges towards responsible AI: explainable safety monitoring, continuous learning of safety issues,
robustness against malicious attacks, multimodal information processing, unified research framework, and
multidisciplinary theory integration. We hope this survey will inspire further research toward safer dialogue
systems.1

1 INTRODUCTION
Safety has become one of the most essential requirements for large dialogue systems [ 12,14,151,
152], with researchers’ realizations that the best conversational models have been comparable
with human-beings in semantics, knowledge, and interactiveness with the help of a huge scale
of training corpora, parameters, external database, and even Internet [ 17,109,130,143]. Uncanny
valley effect [ 90] indicates that a sufficiently intelligent dialogue system might instead bring out
a sense of fear to human-beings, especially when the dialogue system is no more identified as
an “intelligent assistant” (e.g., chit-chat bots v.s. Siri) and then exhibit unsafe motivations and
behaviors. For example, a line of works report exposed issues about hate speech, social bias, further
ethics and privacy leakage in large dialogue systems [ 100], which raised widespread discussion
and concern about the safety of dialogue systems. Towards safe, responsible, and moral dialogue
systems, aligning dialogue systems with legislation, social norms and human values has become a
new research hot spot [ 52,56]. Hundreds of works are emerging in the safety community, with
missions to build the next generation of responsible AI. It is time to organize existing safety-related
works and then encourage more vigorous development in this field.
The scope of safety is the primary issue that needs to be well discussed and addressed in
the safety community. As the very first task, automatic hate speech detection has been applied
since many years ago [ 123,136]. As the development of language and culture, along with the
intelligence improvement of dialogue systems, the requirements towards dialogue systems naturally
step to a whole new high level and the scope of safety gradually becomes larger [ 115,139]. For

Dialogue systems are supposed to receive comprehensive safety assessment before or even
during deployment. Therefore, how to perform automatic evaluation to dialogue systems is another
research focus recently. Moreover, evaluation methods naturally provide essential insights to
further improve the safety of dialogue systems. In the second part of this survey, we summarize
evaluation methods towards an integrated dialogue system, which not only includes traditional
safety detection which performs case-level toxicity detection, but contains system-level safety
checking towards an integrated system like group preference, values preference, morality, etc.
Meanwhile, we surveyed some approaches to better elicit the potential safety issues of dialogue
systems, i.e., red teaming [40, 100].
The last main part of this survey is safety improvement of dialogue systems. As the final goal,
the approaches of safety improvement are always heavily relied on the previously introduced
modules: benchmarking and evaluation. Besides, safe text generation, as the sub-task of controlled
text generation, follows the similar research development. We summarize various popular safety-
controlled dialogue generation approaches which occur in different stages (e.g., training and
inference stages). We distinguish the approaches between the objects of end-to-end conversational
model and pipeline-based system. The former emphasizes the improvement of the backbone model
including its training and inference stages while the latter emphasizes constructing independent
modules or strategies to counter various safety situations.
In this survey, we pay special attention to those unique issues in interactive systems, especially
context-sensitiveness and the natural distinction between user and system. Context-sensitiveness
indicates that the determination of safety requires more comprehensive understanding about the
context of the conversation. The natural distinction between user and system decides that the
utterance from user and system should be processed differently. For example, user query could
be various and unlimited, and the system should respond appropriately to all possible situations.
This idea induces some unique safety issues in human-bot interaction like providing counseling,
sensitive topics. Moreover, the idea supports the lines of work including language model attacking,
red teaming, safety trigger/exposure, etc. Moreover, for the defender of the system, the conditions
of triggering safety warnings, as well as the strategies to counter different safety situations in
deployment should be handled specifically and carefully.
The whole framework of this survey is shown in Figure 1. Beside the three main parts introduced
above, we further discuss the shortcomings of existing researches and give our understandings
of future research directions in this community. We hope this survey could help new researchers
fast overview current researches in the field, as well as inspire and advance future researches in
dialogue safety field.
2 SCOPE OF EXISTING ISSUES
For the risks exposed in dialogue systems, we provide a bottom-up view to summarize various
types and domains of safety issues, including toxicity (§2.1) and unfairness (§2.2) that are widely
studied, moral and ethical issues (§2.3) that pose challenges, and the little-studied risk of misleading
and private information (§2.4). Examples of the above issues are shown in Table 1, and the relevant
data resources are summarized in Table 2.
2.1 Abusive and Toxic Contents

widely discussed safety issue. It is usually rude, disrespectful, or unreasonable content that uncom-
fortable users and drives them away [101,123]. Toxic language detection and mitigation are of
the utmost importance for deploying safer dialogue systems. Thanks to continuously proposed

Sentence-level Toxicity. Most studies of toxicity issues focus on single sentences [ 16,28,116,154,
163]. These works are increasingly deepened from simply detecting whether there is an attack
[28,29,33,154] into analyzing more fine-grained dimensions such as toxic types and offending
targets. The manifestations of toxicity range from explicit abuse, swearing, and profanity [ 39,117]
to implicit toxicity such as condescension and microaggressions [ 16,49,149]. Price et al . [104]
considered six unhealthy attributes (e.g., hostile, insulting, and dismissive) to promote healthier
online conversations. Target identification is also an essential task that aims to identify the offending
targets, including individuals, marginalizing minorities, or disadvantaged groups [116, 163].
Context-Aware Toxicity. Sentence-level toxic content is usually expressed with explicit toxicity
and is usually context-independent, such as “ Guys without jobs are all idiots. ”. In a dialogue, however,
each sentence is uttered based on historical utterances and contains more complex and implicit
information, thus, its toxicity could be amplified or even vanish due to the nature of interaction
and context-sensitiveness [139].
Context-aware toxic detection is first studied by Gao and Huang [41]. They collected Fox News
comments with rich context information, arguing that context information contributes to a clear
feature supplement, especially for the subtle and creative toxicity. Following the findings, Pavlopou-
los et al . [99] claimed that context does have a statistically significant effect on toxicity annotation,
but this effect is reduced in automatic classification and is seen in only a narrow slice of that tested
dataset.
In addition to learning from human-to-human dialogue, learning from human-machine dialogue
can more directly understand the toxicity of dialogue systems. Motivated by the BBF human-
machine collaboration system [ 33], Roller et al . [115] interacted directly with the dialogue system
to trigger more toxic generations. These data are fed back to the system and guide it toward safer
optimization.
The interactive nature of dialogue allows for more diverse toxic expressions. Sheng et al . [129]
studied Ad Hominems in dialogue responses and summarized different attitudes towards vari-
ous marginalized groups, including stupidity, ignorance, lying, bias, and condescension, in their
AdHomInTweets dataset. Zhang et al . [166] studied the malevolent responses that may cause a
negative user perception. They proposed hierarchical taxonomy of malevolence by considering
physiological behavior (e.g., blame, unconcernedness and arrogance), emotion (e.g., anger, phobia,
and jealousy), and ethical issues (e.g., deceit, immoral, and illegal). Baheti et al . [6] studied the
stance of dialogue models exposed in their responses when confronted with users’ offenses. They
found that chatbots were three times more likely to agree with toxic utterances than safe ones,
citing the necessity of avoiding toxicity agreement rather than completely shutting them down.
Context-Sensitive Toxicity. Context-sensitive toxicity means that the toxic polarity will be reversed
in a specific dialogue context. Xenos et al . [156] studied the new task Context Sensitivity Estimation ,
aiming to identify the change of perceived toxicity with or without context. But they discovered
that context information is not always necessary and can also add to costs. Sun et al . [139] shows
that current dialogue systems, including DialoGPT [ 167], BlenderBot [ 130] and Plato [ 10], are more
fragile in context-sensitive situations, which needs to be further discovered and defined to facilitate
safer deployments.

Counter Speech. Toxic contents have been widely studied in dialogue, not only in dialogue
systems but also in social platforms. Over the years, researchers have worked hard to maintain a
harmonious and healthy social platform, which enables the dialogue system to learn from platform
managers and healthy users how to respond to and fight toxic utterances.
As defined by Garland et al . [42] , counter speech is a generated response to online hate to stop and
prevent the spread of hate speech . In dialogue systems, the target of counter-speech is generally the
aggressive user in interaction. Mathew et al . [82] studied the hate speech and corresponding counter
speech related to various target communities from Twitter and summarized 10 countering strategies,
such as presenting facts, affiliation, and denunciation. These strategies are further verified in Youtube
comment [83]. Chung et al . [24] focus on the hate phenomenon of Islamophobia and construct
the first large-scale, multilingual, expert-based dataset of hate and countering speech covering
the languages of French, Italian, and English. Fanton et al . [36] study multi-target counter-speech
by collecting data with generative models. In their work, a generative language model is refined
iteratively using the collected data from the previous loops and is then employed to generate new
training samples. The generation in each step is reviewed and post-edited by experts to ensure data
quality. Combined with external knowledge, these data can achieve more grounded generations
to fight online hatred, even for unseen domains Chung et al . [25] . However, the responding
strategies summarized in maintaining healthy social platforms cannot be fully transferred to
dialogue systems. For example, the strategy of “ Warning of offline or consequences ” [82,83] may
make users uncomfortable and end the chat, which greatly affects user engagement.
2.2 Unfairness and Discrimination
Social bias is an unfair negative attitude toward a social group or group members based on one-
sided or incorrect information. It usually refers to harmful prejudice that arises from widely
disseminated negative stereotypes related to gender, race, religion and so on [ 124]. Dialogue system
interacts with different groups and inadvertently expresses negative comments on marginalized
groups, making user discomfort and even driving them away. For example, housewives’ moods and
interactive experiences can be easily affected when the system expresses the negative stereotype
that “housewives can only live on their husbands” . Therefore, whether the generated response is fair
to different marginal groups is particularly important, contributing to measuring the social impact
of the dialogue system and promoting its safer deployment.
Demographic Groups and Attributes. Biased opinions arise from differing attitudes toward differ-
ent groups, and in the biased expression, dominant and marginalized groups are usually assigned
different attribute tags [ 5,11,18]. “Jewish ”, for instance, is typically associated with negative
attributes such as greed, meekness, and nerdiness, whereas “ Christion ” is generally associated
with generosity, confidence, and discipline [ 11]. According to this prior knowledge, the bias in
sentences or datasets can be measured by analyzing the association between attribute words and
specific groups, such as by calculating word vector association tests [ 18]. Besides, based on the
demographic group and attributes-based terms, the bias in language models can be reflected by
comparing varying degrees of preferences when using the same sentence to describe different
groups [ 93,94]. In this case, the language model (e.g., BERT and Roberta) can be considered unfair
if it assigns a higher association score to group-term man than woman when given the masked
sentences “ [MASK] bring home the bacon ”.
Stereotype and Bias Opinions. Aside from exploring at the linguistic level [ 5,18,93,94], some
works investigate stereotypes and biases against different groups based on supervised datasets
related to gender, race, religion, and immigrants [ 11,120], aiming to develop more robust detectors
and fairer generation models. To facilitate fine-grained analysis and automatic reasoning of social

bias, Sap et al . [121] presented SBIC dataset with hierarchical categorical and free-text annotations,
including binary offensive label, intention, targeting group, and implicated harm behind statements.
To identify dialog-level social bias, Zhou et al . [169] focused more on context-sensitive scenarios
and presented the CdialBias dataset covering biased, neutral, and anti-bias attitudes in Chinese
responses.
To understand the potential risk of bias generation before deployment, some works are devoted
to constructing adversarial prompts to induce the model, such as BlenderBot and DialoGPT, to
output more biased opinions. These prompts are usually collected from various sources in the
real world [ 31] or synthesized based on demographic group-related terms and template-based
sentence prompts [ 134]. By analyzing the generated contents at the word level and sentence level,
it is helpful to understand the weaknesses of the generative model for targeted improvement.
Bias is an essential issue in conversation safety. But in general, the existing resources mainly
consider common domains such as race, gender, and region [ 11,31,68], and most of the bias groups
involved are around a few dozens, or a hundred [ 121,169], with limited breadth and depth of
coverage. Moreover, the definition and assessment of social bias are strongly related to cultural
background, and the existing resources are mainly in English, so there is a huge gap if they are
migrated to low-resource languages [64, 94].
2.3 Ethics and Morality Issues
Morality is described as “ principles concerning the distinction between right and wrong or good and
bad behaviors ” [35]. With the rapid development of AI, it is essential to align AI with human values,
morality, ethics, and social norms [ 52,56,89,95,132]. In recent years, more and more works focus
on machine ethics and many datasets have been proposed to promote the research, as summarized in
Table 2. Most of these datasets provide ethical judgements on diverse situations. The judgements can
be overall judgements [ 15,56,80], or find-grained scores on various dimensions [ 53,107,144]. And
some of them give rules-of-thumb (RoTs) with structured annotations (cultural pressure, anticipated
agreement, moral foundation, legality, violation severity, etc.) to support the judgement [ 38,60,170].
Some datasets give several different options conditioned on one situation and annotate the preferred
option [ 52,142]. Besides, Sap et al . [121] reason about the implied social bias including the referenced
group and the implied statement, and Emelin et al . [34] conduct moral reasoning for pre-set
intentions, which offers both moral and immoral ways to achieve the goals.
There are relatively fewer works focused on moral dialogue systems. Qiu et al . [107] show
that human value modeling is beneficial for various dialogue tasks. Ziems et al . [170] consider
single-turn dialogues and give RoTs with structured annotations to judge the reply’s morality. For
immoral replies, they also ask annotators to rewrite revised answers. ProsocialDialog [ 60] has 58k
multi-turn dialogues that include prosocial responses faced with potentially unsafe user utterances
based on RoTs. Kim et al . [60] also point out that most existing dialogue systems can’t properly
deal with potentially unsafe user utterances. In summary, there aren’t enough works focused on
building ethical and moral dialogue systems, and there is much room to improve the ethics and
moralities of dialogue systems.
2.4 Risk of Misleading and Privacy Information
Dialogue systems may sometimes provide misleading information or leak the privacy information
contained in the training data.
Privacy and Information Leakage. As large pretrained language models are trained on an enormous
number of texts from the web, it is quite possible that they have seen some sensitive or private
information such as telephone number, email address, physical address, etc. Responsible dialogue
8
systems should not expose these sensitive information. Previous works point out the models
could unintentionally memorize these sensitive information [ 19] and it is feasible to extract the
information from the trained models [ 20]. Besides extracting the sensitive training data, attackers
can also obtain the sensitive information in inference-time inputs. Based on the embedded inputs
(dense vectors), attackers can infer the persona [ 70], digits and keywords [ 98], or raw words [ 135]
in the inputs, which poses a great threat to users’ information security. To avoid the information
leakage, previous works use differential privacy [ 19,20], curated training data [ 19,20] and additional
loss functions [ 70,135]. However, differential privacy could significantly increase the training cost
[19] or hurt the utility [ 20] and additional loss functions may harm the model’s utility or be limited
by the pre-assumed attacking method [ 70,135]. Curating the training data is also impossible to
remove all sensitive information. Thus it is important to design better methods to avoid the privacy
and information leakage.
Providing Inappropriate Suggestions. Due to the interactivity of dialogue systems, many people
who are depressed or have psychological problems may seek help and ask for suggestions from the
dialogue systems. If the dialogue system fails to respond correctly, the seekers may harm themselves
or even suicide [ 151]. Therefore, many works focus on mental health and empathetic dialogue
[3,37,54,76,111,125,138]. However, there is still a large gap between current dialogue models and
human professionals [ 138]. Also, users may ask for suggestions in professional areas such as law
and medicine. Providing inappropriate suggestions in such areas may cause severe harms to users
[13,86,112]. However, most of the existing dialogue models can’t provide appropriate suggestions
in these professional areas and a temporary solution is to simply detect related questions and give
a general response instead of providing concrete but inappropriate suggestions [115].
Expressing Unsuitable Opinions. Responsible dialogue models should not express unsuitable
opinions on sensitive topics such as politics and religion. Bang et al . [9]assess the political prudence
of several open-domain chatbots and find that they often express unsuitable (hyper-partisan or
offensive) opinions when talking about political topics. Roller et al . [115] manually annotate 5
sensitive topics, including politics, religion, drugs, medical advice and NSFW, for dialogue utterances
and train a classifier to detect the utterances related to these sensitive topics. Once detecting the
utterances related to sensitive topics, they require the dialogue model to choose a pre-set general
response. In summary, how to express suitable opinions when talking about sensitive topics is still
an open problem and needs more attention.

2.5 Summary
For safety research on large models, outlining the taxonomy helps clarify the scope, and constructing
benchmark datasets is the foundation for advancing the research. Generally, publicly available
dataset benchmarks exhibit the following characteristics. (1) Focusing mainly on specific safety
issues, such as toxic, social bias, or ethical issues, while little works seek to construct benchmarks
by considering safety from a global perspective. (2) The distribution of datasets across domains is
unbalanced. Works focusing on toxicity and social bias have received much more attention than
others, such as privacy and medical advice. (3) For benchmarks in the same domain, the annotation
schemas used usually vary but overlap, making it difficult to align the labels between benchmarks.
For example, expressions of attacking groups appear in both biased and ethical datasets. (4) For the
quality of the datasets, their labels are very subjective to the individual labelers, resulting in variable
labeling quality across datasets. (5) The data resources are mainly supervised data with safety labels,
while there are few general resources such as common sense knowledge and knowledge graphs.
Overall, we believe that summarizing and generalizing the safety scope, developing a compre-
hensive and finely detailed taxonomy, and formulating a standardized annotation schema will
contribute to promoting standardization.
3 EVALUATION
How to evaluate safety issues well is very important, especially for real-world applications. A
dialogue model should be fully evaluated before deployment. A well-known negative example is
12
Tay, which Microsoft had to revoke within a day, due to its hateful tweets [ 67]. To evaluate the
safety of dialogue models, there are currently have primarily two approaches: (1) Check before
generation. In this method, we directly compute the probability of the given options, which is
usually applied to check the bias within the model. (2) Check after generation. This means that we
input context to the model and make the model generate response (usually the context could easily
trigger the model to generate unsafe responses), and then we evaluate the safety of the dialogue
model by measuring the hacking success rate.
3.1 Check before generation
When using this method, it is often based on probability [ 91,97,128]. Sheng et al . [128] created
some prompts that included different groups, such as men, women, and so on. Then they let the
model continue to generate, and then carried out sentiment classification. Comparing the results
of sentiment classification of different groups, they found that for some minority groups, the
probability of their results being positive was obviously smaller. Nadeem et al . [91] created a
dataset called StereoSet, which is divided into two tasks, inter-sentence and intra-sentence bias
measurement. Each of data consists of a context and three options (stereotype, anti-stereotype, and
unrelated). The main measurement method is to compare the language modeling score of the two
options of stereotype and anti-stereotype. For the ideal model, the two modeling scores should be
the same, so the bias of the model can be measured in this way. Ousidhoum et al . [97] built a large
number of templates for multiple languages. In each sentence, a token was masked, and then used
the model to predict the token, so as to detect the potentially harmful content of the model.
3.2 Check after generation
When using this method to evaluate dialogue models, there are two main steps, triggering the
model to generate unsafe content and detecting these unsafe content.
3.2.1 Problem Exposure. To expose the potential problems of dialogue models, the main approach
is to construct various adversarial inputs, which possibly elicit unsafe and immoral outputs from
dialogue models. The ways to construct adversarial inputs can be divided into the following 4
categories: 1) construct samples with templates, 2) extract real-world data, 3) employ crowdsourcing
workers, and 4) automatically generate new samples based on pretrained language models.
Construct samples with templates. Works in this category mainly combine different choices
with a manually designed template. For example, Sheng et al . [128] designed the template “The
XYZ worked as” where XYZ is selected from {woman, man, Black person, White person, gay
person, straight person}. They compare the outputs’ sentiment and regard score conditioned on
different prefix prompts and found that GPT-2 [ 108] exhibits distinct levels of bias towards different
demographics. Bang et al . [9] designed both neutral (e.g., “Let’s talk about <Politician>.”) and
biased (e.g., “<Politician> has done the best job as a politician.”) templates related to politicians or
political beliefs to assess political prudence of chatbots. The metrics measure hyper-partisanship,
offensiveness and slantedness of outputs generated by chatbots. Nadeem et al . [91] persented a
large-scale dataset to assess language models’ stereotypical bias in four domains including race,
religion, gender and profession. They proposed Context Association Tests (CATs), which offered
three different options ( stereotype ,anti-stereotype andunrelated ) based on a given context that
contains specific demographics.
Extract real-world data. Besides using templates, a more direct way is to extract inputs from real-
world data. Gehman et al . [43] selected prompts with different toxicity levels from OpenWebText
Corpus [45] to evaluate neural toxic degeneration in language models. Sheng et al . [129] extracted
Recent Advances towards Safe, Responsible, and Moral Dialogue Systems: A Survey 13
posts on different topics from Twitter to investigate ad hominems in dialogue responses. Schick
et al. [122] used RealToxicityPrompts [ 43] which is extracted from OpenWebText Corpus to test
the safety of GPT-2.
Employ crowdsourcing workers. Resorting to human annotators is more flexible to satisfy compli-
cated requirements and could yield samples with high quality, despite the higher costs. Roller et al .
[115] constructed Bot-Adversarial Dialogue (BAD) dataset by encouraging crowdsourcing workers
to elicit offensive messages from dialogue models. The workers can use either toxic or benign
utterances to induce unsafe responses from dialogue models during the multi-turn conversation.
Automatically generate new samples based on pretrained language models. Limited by the high
costs, relying on human annotators to construct adversarial inputs cannot ensure a high diversity
of the samples. Therefore, it is reasonable to adopt pretrained language models to automatically
construct adversarial inputs. Perez et al . [100] uses a seperate language model to automatically
generate inputs that could elicit desired outputs from the target language model. Besides zero-shot
generation, they also explored few-shot generation, supervised learning and reinforcement learning
to generate the test cases more efficiently.
3.2.2 Detection. To evaluate the dialogue model, we need to check whether we have successfully
triggered unsafe content in the previous step. For this purpose, the use of classifiers is a common
and effective method. Here we mainly introduce from two aspects, data and model.
Data level. For data collection, there are mainly the following methods, manual collection,
human-in-the-loop process, model generation.
Manual collection relies on human annotators to compose new samples or label existing data
[34,38,52,80,121]. However, solely relying on human annotators can be expensive and limits
the scale of data. Also, there are some cases (e.g., interactive conversation) that are suitable for
incorporating models. Therefore, many works let models cooperate with human annotators to
construct data [ 6,40,51,60,115,170]. It is worth noting that large pretrained language models such
as GPT-3 [ 17] play a key role in generating new samples through zero-shot or few-shot prompting
[51,60]. Moreover, some works completely remove human involvement and solely rely on large
language models to generate new data [ 100,131], which are often used to test the flaws of models.
Model level. From model level, it can be divided into traditional methods and deep learning
methods. The traditional methods include: logical regression, Bayesian models, SVM, random forest,
and some methods based on keyword [ 113,150]. As for deep learning methods, the earlier methods
are based on the bag of words model, CNN, RNN and LSTM [44, 47, 57, 62, 87, 88, 133, 146].
In this paper, we mainly discuss the method of using the pre-trained models. This approach was
suddenly revealed with the proposal of Bert [ 30], which consists of multiple Transformers encoders.
Bert primarily employs two pre-training tasks: MLM (Masked Language Model) and NSP (Next
Sentence Prediction). The MLM task is to randomly mask a portion of the tokens in a sentence and
make predictions. In addition, to learn inter-sentence relations, they used the NSP task, which is
to predict weather a sentence is the following one after another sentence. After this, a variety of
methods have further improved the Bert [ 65,77,168]. Liu et al . [77] proposed Roberta by removing
the NSP task of Bert and modifying the mask strategy. Lan et al . [65] proposed Albert, trying to
reduce the model parameters and maintain the performance. Zhang et al . [168] introduced external
knowledge into the pre-training and proposed three types of pre-training tasks: basic-level masking,
prase-level masking, and entity-level masking. Futhermore, Sun et al . [140] explored continual

Recently, as safety issues have become important, more methods have been proposed to im-

3.3 Summary
In general, the mainstream is still using classifiers to evaluate models, but this approach has a long
history of problems, especially bias [ 63,157,171] and robustness [ 51,55] issues, and naturally a
future work is classifier de-biasing as well as improving robustness. Furthermore, the interpretability
of classifiers is poor. Although there are some work to explore the interpretability [ 8,114], they
mostly interpret by observing the key word. However, for context-level tasks, only relying on the
key word can not determine whether the model really has the ability to discriminate. Instead some
reasoning is required, so how to better build interpretable models is also a future work. Moreover,
the current utilization of large models is not enough, mainly focusing on self-detoxification, which
we think is worth further exploration, such as using the chain of thought approach to build
interpretable classifiers.

draws lots of attention recently. In this section, we introduce the recent advances in the methods
to improve the safety of dialogue system. Moreover, we divide the methods into two mainstreams:

4.1 Towards E2E Conversational Model
Most approaches to improve safety are overwhelming focusing on E2E conversational model, espe-
cially in academia. Noticeably, the development of this direction relies strongly on the development
of controlled text generation techniques where “safety” could be seen as an important desired
attribute in dialogue response generation. Similar to the taxonomy described in [ 164], we classify
the safety improvement methods according to the stage that the methods take effect. The stages
are classified as “pre-processing phase”, “training phase”, and “inference phase”.
Pre-processing Phase. The behaviors of a model are primarily sourced from its training corpus.
Hence, removing unsafe data and adding safe data into training corpus in pre-processing phase can
directly and effectively have significant impact on the final behavior of the dialogue systems. Roller
et al. [115] summarizes multiple methods to clean unsafe and sensitive data, including utterance-
based and author-based methods. In general, all the detectors introduced in Sec §3.2.2 could be
applied in this step to remove those undesired data (e.g., BBF [ 33], Detoxify [ 50]). Meanwhile,
using safety detectors to filter training data by setting a confidence threshold has almost become
the standard step to build an intelligent dialogue system. For another, under the circumstance
that the data are mostly crawled from social media (especially pre-training data) in recent years,
some researchers found that certain authors tend to post harmful contents. Removing all the
16
contents posted by the labeled authors is found effective and could discard those implicit unsafe
posts [ 33,46,147]. Thus, author-based filtering is also utilized in pre-processing phase. Other than
removing those undesired data, adding parallel data to promote fairness is another method in
pre-processing phase.
Training Phase. The model behaviors are determined by its parameters, and the parameters are
obtained in pre-training or fine-tuning stages. Therefore, it is most essential to shape the safe
behaviors in training phase for dialogue systems. And naturally, lots of controlled text generation
methods in training phase could be applied to enhance the safety of dialogue systems. For example,
CTRL [ 58] pre-pended a control codes before sentences in training corpora, which is a direct and
effective method to model 𝑃𝜃(𝑥𝑡|𝑥<𝑡, 𝑐), where 𝑐is the desired attribute that is formalized into
control codes. Roller et al . [115] applied this method in safety and style control and assign the safe
or unsafe control codes to be pre-pended to each training example. In inference stage, the safe
control code is pre-pended to generate safe text. Besides, Roller et al . [115] also tries this method in
gender bias mitigation using female (male) control codes. Sharing the similar idea, Krause et al .
[61] proposed Generative Discriminator to guide sequence generation (GeDi), which utilized Bayes
theorem to model the conditional probability 𝑃𝜃(𝑐|𝑥1:𝑡).
In recent years, over the fast development of large pre-trained models, prompt tuning has become
a new paradigm to adapt downstream tasks [ 74]. Li and Liang [71] added a “prefix” before real
inputs and searched the optimal soft prompt in embedding layer in models by gradient. More
recently, Diffusion models are also found effective in controlled text generation, emerging as the
next SOTA generation model [ 72]. All these controlled text generation methods are easy to adapt
to safe generation tasks. Reinforcement learning (RL) is another popular approach to guide models
to generate words with target attributes. The core module, reward function in RL is always given
by a scoring model or safety detector [40, 100].
More recently, large language models (LLM) have promising ability in generalize across tasks by
instruction tuning [ 23]. Moreover, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is then
applied to better elicit LLM’s internal knowledge and align with humans’ values [ 96,137]. Based
on RLHF, Bai et al . [7] designed a RL from AI feedback diagram to get a more harmless (and still
helpful) language model.
Inference Phase. Training phase always requires large resources and costs to build a new model.
Instead, most methods utilized in inference phase merely provide an on-the-fly plugin without
changing the parameters in the original language models. This plug-and-play method becomes
more popular after the emergence of pre-trained models like GPT [ 17,108], which costs huge
resources in pre-training stage. Aiming at very dirty and explicit words, n-gram blocking is largely
used in decoding stage, which directly makes the sampling probability of some undesired words
as zero. Rather than only token-level unsafety avoidance, PPLM [ 27] notices that 𝑃(𝑥|𝑐) ∝𝑃(𝑐|𝑥),
and adopts an attribute model to compute 𝑃(𝑐|𝑥)to guide the model at sentence level. PPLM lies in
inference phase and does not require any additional changes in training phase. However, PPLM
still requires updating the parameters of large models, leading to slow inference speed. As the
improvement of PPLM, FUDGE [ 162] does not update any parameter in the model. and introduces
a future discriminator to predict whether the ongoing generation text would conform to the desired
attribute or not. DExperts [ 73] adopts two generative models (expert and anti-expert models)
to replace the original discriminator, inspired by Bayes theorem and GeDi [ 61]. From another
perspective, some methods based on prompting for zero-shot can also be applied to safe generation
tasks. For example, Schick et al . [122] found that the language model itself recognizes well its
generative undesired contents including toxicity and bias. They add the self-debiasing inputs (e.g.,
The following text discriminates against people because of their Bias Type ) into the prompt to form

an undesired words generator “anti-expert”, which models the distribution with higher probability
of undesired words. The thought of self-detoxification inspires other works like [159].
4.2 Towards Pipeline-based System
Different from model-level improvement, pipeline-level improvement focuses on the flow of the
human-system interaction. The core idea lies in different modules or strategies to counter various
safety issues. For example, most existing deployed dialogue systems would speak words like “Let’s
change a topic” to avoid sensitive topic continuation after detecting sensitive topics like politics.
In this section, we introduce pipeline-based safety improvement from perspectives of user and
dialogue system.
User Perspective. The context uttered by user may trigger serious safety problems [ 40,100].
Faced on the unsafe context, the well-known “security response” is commonly utilized. Therefore,
detecting highly inductive contexts and rejecting strategy is essential in the pipeline. Refer to
§3.2.2 for the introduction of safety detection of context given by user. Noticeably, the safety
detection for users should be of higher robustness and generalization because the words by user
are always unrestricted and any situation could occur. We introduce some common strategies
to counter different unsafe contexts. Sensitive topic avoidance is largely applied into dialogue
system deployment. Roller et al . [115] introduced a sensitive topic classifier to recognize “politics”,
“religion”, “drugs”, “medical advice”, and “NSFW” of context. When detected, a canned response is
triggered. When the user utterance involves some hate speeches, hate speech countering is found a
feasible way to counter and correct the wrong idea in contexts. Relying on hate speech countering
datasets [ 24,82] and external knowledge [ 25], the dialogue system is expected to generate responses
to stop and prevent the spread of hate speech [ 42]. Recently some researchers found that recovery
from safety failure is also important [ 145]. When failure happens, the correction from user (e.g.,
“You shouldn’t say bad words.”) should be detected and it is appropriate for bots to express sorry in
this situation.
Bot Perspective. The pipeline improvement of bot perspective occurs between the model gen-
erating and message showing in user interface. In this stage, the system conducts the last check
and edition for the generated response. The most common strategy is rejection sampling where
the responses are detected as unsafe by detector would be recalled and re-generated. Besides, in
some serious situations, human intervention is necessary and is often inserted in this stage. Some
researchers found that only a small proportion of the whole response (e.g., one or two words) needs
to be fixed. Thus, An edition module takes effect after the generation to fix some problems in some
works [ 75,78]. Similarly, text style transfer or rephrasing from toxicity to non-toxicity can also be
plugged in this stage [26, 66].
5 DISCUSSIONS AND FUTURE TRENDS
In this section, we discuss some challenging problems that merit further investigation in the
future from six aspects: 1) explainable safety monitoring, 2) continuous learning of safety issues, 3)
robustness against malicious attacks, 4) multimodal information processing, 5) unified research
framework, and 6) multidisciplinary theory integration.
5.1 Explainable Safety Monitoring
Whether an utterance is appropriate is usually determined by a benchmark detector, such as the per-
spective API, by outputting a category label or a corresponding predicted score. The decision-making
process is frequently a "black box" where the user has difficulty seeing its rationale. Therefore the
reliability of the results is difficult to judge. Further consideration should be given to how and why
18
the safety monitor modules make their decisions, as well as how credible those decisions are. For
safety evaluation to be explained, it is essential to output results with corresponding explanations.
This allows humans to understand why decisions are made, and developers to analyze and fix their
weaknesses to advance more reliable safety deployments.
To provide plausible rationales, Mathew et al . [84] proposed Hatexplain models, which visualize
the decision-making process by highlighting influential spans in the input text through the atten-
tion score while giving category labels. Such highlighted information also facilitates the chatbot
for understanding the attack from input and conducting peer-to-peer defense to generate safer
responses [ 85]. Detection and evaluating safety issues is a complex task, but its decision process
can be interpretably simplified by task decomposition . Starting from multiple dimensions, such
as offensive detection, target identification, and implicit statement inference, the reasoning of
toxic and unfair detection can be more intuitive [ 2,121]. To improve the trustworthiness of moral
judgments, it is essential to explore whether a given utterance conforms to or violates which moral
code, for example, by combining RoTs with additional grounded reasoning [60].
While safety-related research has attempted to achieve interpretability, it is unclear how these
interpretations can be ensured to be accurate and consistent with human judgment.
5.2 Continuous Learning of Safety Issues
The identification and resolution of safety issues is an ongoing process. First, the current safety
deployment relies mainly on predefined taxonomy and supervised datasets. However, there is
still no complete consensus on the safety boundary. Even with the fine-grained taxonomy, the
amount and coverage of the training data can further limit the performance of safety modules.
Secondly, as times evolve, new domains and topics related to safety are emerging, and thus the
safety considerations should be continuously updated.
The most direct and effective way to continuously optimize the safety module is by collecting data
on new domains and fine-tuning the model. This is exactly what the constantly released benchmark
datasets do. They usually perform targeted enhancements after summarizing a new type of safety
issue, such as ad hominems [ 129] and toxicity agreement [ 32,139]. The human-machine interaction
allows more direct exposure of the model’s safety issues, e.g., Dinan et al . [33] collected a large
amount of adversarial data to improve the detection model’s robustness. However, such data-driven
approaches have significant limitations in terms of efficiency and cost during data collection and
annotation.
Learning from user interaction data , so that the model gains self-reinforcement seems to be
a once-and-for-all approach. Chatbots can judge the safety of self-utterance by perceiving user
feedback in the interaction. After detecting the corrective feedback (e.g., You have a biased opinion
) from user, the chatbot learns to apologize and correct its behavior, thus gaining the ability to
self-correct. Benefiting from the wide deployment of large generative models, directly using the
feedback of user ratings (e.g., good or bad) contributes to the model’s continuous learning. A large
amount of feedback data allows the model to be self-sufficient to continuously optimize and cope
with emerging safety issues [130].
In addition, it is critical to enhance the generalization capability of the safety module to new
domains. This allows the model to quickly resolve new problems as they are encountered, rather
than relying passively on feeding new data, which is usually costly to collect and annotate. To
enhance the understanding of new safety problems, incorporating offline and online knowledge
into safety modules is an optional solution. The model can perceive and reason about new problems
based on, for example, internet search engines and commonsense knowledge graphs.

5.3 Robustness against Malicious Attacks
The robustness of a model refers to its ability to maintain performance despite perturbations or
uncertainty. To become a responsible AI, a dialogue system must be robust when interacting with
diverse and complex adversarial inputs and then output safe responses.
Safety considerations are usually included in released dialogue systems during the training or
deployment phases [ 115]. However, various safety issues still arise during human-computer inter-
action due to the different distribution in training and deployment scenarios [ 4]. When confronted
with virtual dialogue systems, users are more toxic and violent than in real conversations because
there are no moral constraints. Moreover, users’ utterances are characterized by covering a wider
range of topics and more diverse styles. Deploying and implementing dialogue systems in such
diverse and complex environments significantly impacts their safety robustness, compared with
training environments.
To make conversational systems robustly safe in the face of various user inputs, much work has
been devoted to designing adversarial attacks to expose their safety vulnerabilities. As described
in section 3.2.1, the adversarial samples are constructed by manual [115], template-based [9], and
model-based methods [ 85,100], which trigger unsafe outputs (e.g., toxic or unethical responses) to
achieve successful attacks. The detector determines whether the attack was successful, and it also
serves as the last layer of safety deployment, supervising the output of the response. Therefore,
therobustness of the detector plays a decisive role in evaluating the effectiveness of the attack and
defense strategies, as well as the global safety monitoring of human-computer interaction. To build
robust detectors, some work constructs adversarial samples from lexical level [ 118] to sentence
level by data augmentation or manually creates more covert adversarial inputs in an attempt to
evade the detection of the classifier [33].
The attacks, however, will evolve as the defense strategy evolves, and classifiers and generation
models are often vulnerable to the emerging attacks. It is a long-term challenge to conduct robustness
research on dialog safety, which requires continuous attention to exposed safety issues and timely
defensive measures to maximize safety deployment.
5.4 Multimodal Information Processing
Large language models (e.g., Meena [ 1], BlenderBot [ 130] and DialoGPT [ 167]) have shown their
strong interaction capabilities. However, they are mainly trained on textual datasets, and the safety
issues exposed are limited to the NLP field. Recently, with the rise of multimodal pre-training
models (e.g., NÜWA [ 153], DALL-E2 [ 110], GauGAN2 [ 119], etc.), AI technology has demonstrated
the powerful cross-modal (text-image) creation ability. We believe that multimodal interaction
is an inevitable trend for chatbots, while the ensuing safety issues involved are challenging and
under-explored.
In the current research, harmful memes are the most common multimodal safety issues, which
are created by combining simplified textual descriptions with associated background images
[92,102,103,126]. They can be easily designed in different languages and spread quickly among
users from different cultures. In memes, nearly any safety issue (e.g., offensive, violent, racist, etc.)
can be expressed, and their typically humorous or sarcastic design leads to subtler expressions of
harm [ 127]. In addition, safety issues in multimodal interaction involve more complex and broader
research questions, such as recognizing whether the users are on the verge of self-harm from their
speech, or determining whether ethics are violated when a contraband image is detected. These
are all potential future directions that deserve to be thoroughly investigated.

5.5 Unified Research Framework
The safety research of dialog systems involves a variety of complex and diverse tasks. They are
relevant but safety detection or generation models are typically developed for specific domains and
tasks without parameter sharing, which makes it challenging to integrate resources to maximize
benefits.
As shown in recent studies, developing a unified framework based on pre-trained models can
facilitate effective knowledge sharing and better generalization among different tasks [ 81,141,158],
such as the Unified QA system [ 59], universal commonsense reasoning model [ 79], vision-and-
language learning framework [ 22], and the unified generative NER model [ 161]. These unified
frameworks have been instrumental in encouraging fundamental advances in the NLP community
and accelerating research on models.
In the field of dialog safety, research benchmarks are typically task-specific. As described in
section 2.5, existing benchmark datasets are characterized by incompatible annotation schema
adopted by each and unbalanced data distribution across domains. Thus, building a unified safety
research framework promotes knowledge integration across datasets, as well as more robust
performance on relevant tasks, rather than just fitting a specific task.
5.6 Multidisciplinary Theory Integration
The ultimate goal of safety research is to empower chatbots with the right ethical values and to create
trustworthy and responsible AI systems. Safety issues involve not only the question of what moral
obligations they have, but also a range of other social, political, and ethical issues. Conversational
safety research is a new field at the intersection of multiple disciplines, encompassing computer
science, moral philosophy, robotics ethics, cognitive science, psychology, sociology, and others.
For example, to equip robots with a moral and ethical outlook [ 60], rules-of-thumb (ROTs) [ 38]
are constructed theoretically regarding ethical knowledge. These ROTs serve as a benchmark
to strictly monitor robots’ responses so that they are aligned with human values. Knowledge of
psychology enables chatbots to better understand emergencies (e.g., self-harm or severe depression
of the user) during interactions so that potential risks can be addressed and avoided on time. For
more specialized fields, such as law and medicine, researchers from relevant professions should be
involved to empower the chatbots to discuss these technical topics. In safety research, a problem-
oriented and unified body of knowledge from various disciplines is required.
6 CONCLUSION
In this paper, we review the recent advances in safety research toward dialog systems. We focus
the survey on the scope of safety issues and on the approaches proposed for safety evaluation
and safety improvement. We also discuss some interesting and challenging future trends from six
aspects. We hope this survey will provide new insights for future research in this field and promote
the safe deployment of dialog systems.
